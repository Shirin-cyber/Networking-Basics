## 2.3.1 ISP services

An **Internet Service Provider (ISP)** is the link between my home network and the internet. So it's basically the bridge between my little network and the "network of networks" from Module 1.

What can count as an ISP? More than I expected:

- the local **cable** provider
- a **landline telephone** service provider
- the **cellular network** that gives me my smartphone service
- an **independent provider** that **leases bandwidth** on another company's physical network infrastructure

Two things stood out to me here:

- **My mobile carrier is an ISP too.** I'd never really thought of it that way.
- ISPs don't always own the wires. An independent provider can just rent bandwidth on someone else's network. And "bandwidth" is the word from 1.3, so it means they're renting capacity.

### Extra services

Lots of ISPs also offer extra stuff to people on a contract, like:

- email accounts
- network storage
- website hosting
- automated backup or security services

### How ISPs connect to each other

ISPs are critical to communication across the global internet. Each ISP connects to other ISPs, creating a network of links that connects users all over the world. They're connected in a **hierarchy**, which makes sure internet traffic generally takes the **shortest path** from source to destination. (That probably helps with latency from 1.3 too, since a shorter path should mean fewer devices and fewer delays.)

### The internet backbone

The **internet backbone** is like an **information super highway**: high-speed data links connecting service provider networks in major cities around the world.

- Its main medium is **fiber-optic cable**.
- The cable is usually buried **underground** to connect cities within a continent.
- It also runs **under the sea** to connect continents, countries, and cities.

**Connecting the dots:** back in 1.2.3 it said larger networks use fiber to carry signals reliably over long distances. The backbone is the biggest example of that. It's the long-distance part of the internet, and it's made of light moving through glass cables under the ocean.

## 2.3.2 ISP connections

The backbone is a complicated web of fiber-optic cables, with expensive **switches and routers** directing information from source to destination hosts. Most home users never see any of that. For them, connecting to the ISP is pretty simple.

### Two ways to connect at home

| Option | Setup | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Modem only** | A modem gives a computer a direct connection to the ISP | **Don't use it.** The computer isn't protected on the internet. |
| **Wireless integrated router** | A router connects to the ISP, and my devices connect to the router | **Most common**, and the secure option |

### What's inside the wireless integrated router

The router is the one that securely connects a computer to the ISP. It's one box, but it includes:

- a **switch**, to connect wired hosts
- a **wireless AP (access point)**, to connect wireless hosts
- **client IP addressing information** for the hosts inside
- **security** for the hosts inside

**Connecting the dots:** this is where it clicks. In 2.2.1 the intermediate devices were router, switch, and wireless access point, as if they were three separate things. But a home wireless router is **all three in one box**. So I'm now fairly sure the "box in the corner" from Kishori's living room (and from my own home) is exactly this.

## 2.3.3 Cable and DSL connections

Most home users don't actually get fiber all the way into their homes. The two most common options are **cable** and **DSL**.

### Cable

- Usually offered by **cable TV providers**.
- The internet data signal travels on the **same coaxial cable** that carries the TV signal.
- It's **high bandwidth** and **always on**.
- A special **cable modem** separates the internet signal from the other signals on the cable, then provides an **Ethernet connection** to a host computer or LAN.

### DSL (Digital Subscriber Line)

- Also **high bandwidth** and **always on**.
- Needs a special high-speed **DSL modem** that separates the DSL signal from the telephone signal, then provides an **Ethernet connection** to a host computer or LAN.
- It runs over a **telephone line**, and that line is split into **three channels**:

| Channel | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Voice** | Phone calls. I can get calls without disconnecting from the internet. |
| **Download** | The faster one, for receiving information from the internet |
| **Upload** | For sending information. Usually slightly slower than the download channel. |

- **Speed and quality** depend mainly on the **quality of the phone line** and the **distance from the phone company's central office**. The farther away I am, the slower it is.

**Connecting the dots:** download being faster than upload is the same thing from the speed test in 1.3.3, where the download number was a bit higher than the upload number. So that's not random. It's built into how DSL splits up the line.

## 2.3.4 Additional connectivity options

| Option | How it works | Good | Not so good |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cellular** | Uses a cell phone network. Anywhere I get a signal, I can get internet. | Great where there's no other internet, or if I'm always on the move | Performance is limited by the phone and the cell tower. The carrier usually **meters** the data and can charge extra for going over the plan. |
| **Satellite** | A satellite dish connects to the satellite | Good for homes or offices that can't get DSL or cable. Speeds are generally good. Useful where there's no other internet. | The dish needs a **clear line of sight** to the satellite, so heavy trees or overhead obstructions are a problem. Equipment and install can be pricey (check for deals), plus a moderate monthly fee. |
| **Dial-up** | Uses any phone line and a modem. I call the ISP's access number to connect. | Cheap, and useful for mobile access while traveling | **Low bandwidth**, usually not enough for big data transfers. Only use it when faster options aren't available. |
| **Fiber (metro areas)** | In cities, many apartments and small offices are connected **directly with fiber-optic cable** | The ISP can offer higher bandwidth and more services at once, like internet, phone, and TV | Depends on where I live |

The text had a heading that said "Satellite Connection" right before the fiber part, but the paragraph is clearly about fiber, so I filed it under fiber.

### Which one do I get?

It depends on **where I live** and **which providers are available** there. So it's less "which is best" and more "which one can I actually get."

## What I want to remember

- An **ISP** links my home network to the internet. Cable companies, phone companies, cellular carriers, and independent providers can all be ISPs.
- ISPs connect to each other in a **hierarchy**, and the **internet backbone** is made of **fiber-optic cable**, underground and under the sea.
- A **modem alone** isn't safe. A **wireless integrated router** (router + switch + wireless AP in one box) is the common and secure setup.
- **Cable** uses the TV coaxial line and **DSL** uses the phone line, split into voice, download, and upload channels. DSL gets slower the farther I am from the central office.
- Other options: **cellular** (metered data), **satellite** (needs a clear line of sight), **dial-up** (slow, last resort), and **fiber** (fast, in cities).
- What's available depends on **where I live**.
