## 5.0 Introduction

### 5.0.1 Why should I take this module?

It's the next day, and Kishori has a new patient, **Srinivas**, who was just admitted to a room. This story is a good one because it's really about language.

- Srinivas is from **Narayanpet** and speaks **Telugu**.
- Kishori speaks **Marathi**.
- Those two Indian languages are **very different**, so neither of them speaks the other's native language.
- But they **both speak English**, so they decide to talk in English.

The point is that before two people even start communicating, they set up some **rules or agreements** to govern the conversation. For example:

- What **method** of communication will we use?
- What **language** will we use?
- Do we need to **confirm that the message was received**? For example, Kishori might have Srinivas **sign a document** to show he understood her care instructions.

Networks need the same thing. They need **rules**, called **protocols**, so communication actually works. This module covers the **communication principles** for networks.

**Connecting the dots:**

- Kishori and Srinivas speaking English is basically what the **standards** in Module 4 do for devices. A phone from one company and a router from another company don't speak the same "native language," but if both follow the same Wi-Fi standard (802.11), they can talk. English is their shared standard.
- The signed document is a way to **confirm a message was received and understood**. I'm guessing networks have something like that too, but the course will have to tell me how.
- This goes all the way back to the Module 1 intro, where Kishori's hospital devices didn't always communicate. A good guess is that devices need to **agree on the rules** first, just like two people need a shared language. And 3.1.1 mentioned that Bluetooth works with **different rules and protocols** than Wi-Fi, which is the same idea.
