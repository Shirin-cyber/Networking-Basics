## 4.2.1 LAN wireless frequencies

The wireless technologies used most in home networks live in two **unlicensed** frequency ranges: **2.4 GHz** and **5 GHz**.

"Unlicensed" just means those parts of the electromagnetic spectrum can be used **without a permit**. So I don't need special permission to use my own Wi-Fi. (GHz is gigahertz, a measure of frequency. The course doesn't define it here, but I'm pretty sure it means billions of cycles per second.)

Two different technologies share these bands:

### Bluetooth

- Uses the **2.4 GHz** band.
- Limited to **low speed** and **short range**.
- But it can talk to **many devices at the same time**. That one-to-many setup is why it's the go-to for **computer peripherals** like wireless mice, keyboards, and printers.
- Also a good way to send **audio** to speakers or headphones.

### 802.11 wireless LANs

- Uses both **2.4 GHz and 5 GHz**.
- This covers the modern wireless LAN technologies that follow the various **IEEE 802.11 standards**.
- Unlike Bluetooth, 802.11 devices transmit at a **much higher power level**, so they get **greater range** and **better throughput**.

| | Bluetooth | 802.11 |
|---|---|---|
| **Bands** | 2.4 GHz | 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz |
| **Speed and range** | Low speed, short range | Greater range and better throughput |
| **Power** | Lower | Much higher |
| **Good at** | Talking to many devices at once (mice, keyboards, printers, audio) | Connecting devices to a LAN and the internet |

**Connecting the dots:**

- This matches 3.1.3, where Bluetooth was low-power and short range. Now I know the reason 802.11 can reach further is simply that it transmits at a higher power.
- "Throughput" is the word from 1.3, so this is saying 802.11 gets more real-world data across.

## 4.2.2 Wired network technologies

Lots of home devices support wireless, but some things are still better with a **wired switch connection**, one that isn't **shared with other users** on the network.

### Ethernet

The most common wired protocol is **Ethernet**. It's really a **suite of protocols** that lets network devices communicate over a wired LAN. And an Ethernet LAN can use **many different types of wiring media**.

### How devices get connected

- **Directly connected devices** use an **Ethernet patch cable**, usually **unshielded twisted pair (UTP)**. They come in different lengths, and you can buy them with **RJ-45 connectors** already attached.
- **Newer homes** may already have **Ethernet jacks wired into the walls**.
- **Homes without UTP wiring** have other options, like **powerline**, that can spread wired connectivity around the house. (The course doesn't explain powerline here. I think it uses the home's electrical wiring, but that's my guess.)

### The 3 types of cable

| Cable | What it is | Good to know |
|---|---|---|
| **Category 5e (UTP)** | **4 pairs of wires**, twisted to reduce electrical interference | The **most common** wiring in a LAN |
| **Coaxial cable** | An **inner wire**, surrounded by a **tubular insulating layer**, then a **tubular conducting shield**. Most also have an outer insulating jacket. | This is the same kind of cable that carries cable TV and internet from a cable provider (see 2.3.3) |
| **Fiber-optic cable** | **Glass or plastic**, about as thin as a **human hair** | Carries digital info at **very high speeds** over **long distances**, with very **high bandwidth** so it can carry huge amounts of data |

## What I want to remember

- Home wireless uses the **unlicensed 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz** bands, which means no permit is needed.
- **Bluetooth** (2.4 GHz) is low speed and short range, but it can connect many devices at once. It's great for peripherals and audio.
- **802.11** devices (2.4 and 5 GHz) use higher power, so they get better range and throughput.
- **Ethernet** is the most common wired protocol, and it works over a few different media.
- **UTP** patch cables with **RJ-45** connectors connect devices directly. **Category 5e** (4 twisted pairs) is the most common. **Powerline** is an option if the house has no Ethernet wiring.
- **Coax** has an inner wire, an insulating layer, and a conducting shield. **Fiber** is glass or plastic, as thin as a hair, with very high bandwidth over long distances.
