## 4.1.1 Video: Typical home network setup

This video walks through the equipment in a normal home network, and it finally explains what the "box in the corner" is made of.

### A home network is really two networks

Most home networks have **at least two separate networks**:

1. **The public network** coming in from the service provider (usually a **cable** or **DSL** provider).
2. **My local home network.**

And here's the catch: the provider's network **doesn't use the same protocols and transmission methods** as an Ethernet network. They speak different languages.

### So that's why there's a modem

Almost every home network has some kind of **modem**. Its job is to **convert the signals** coming in from the provider into something my home network can use.

In the video, the example is a **cable TV modem**:

- It has an **input** for the coaxial cable from the cable company.
- It has an **output port** that goes to the local home network.

**Connecting the dots:** this is the same modem from 2.3.3. There it said a cable modem separates the internet signal from the other signals on the cable and gives me an **Ethernet connection**. Now I get *why* it's needed: the two sides don't speak the same language, so something has to translate.

### Some devices have the modem built in

Some home networking devices come with the **modem built in**. The one in the video has a connector for the cable company, and then the usual LAN outputs on the other side.

Almost all home networking devices also offer **wireless**. Here's what the device in the video has:

- A **yellow port marked "internet"**, which is the connection to the provider. (On the one with the modem built in, it doesn't need a separate modem plugged in.)
- **LAN ports**, which are usually **switched ports**, for plugging in devices.
- A **built-in access point** for wireless.

Home networking devices from **any vendor** are set up pretty much the same way: an **internet port**, plus **LAN ports**.

### Why they're called "routers"

These devices are usually called **wireless routers**, and the reason they're called routers is that the ports are split into **two separate networks**:

- The **local network**: wired and wireless devices.
- The network on the **internet side**.

On the local side, I can plug a device into an Ethernet port with a cable, and all those devices are on the **same network**. And wireless devices usually join that **same local network** as the wired ones.

So if I check the IP addresses on my home network, I'd see the wireless users getting an IP address on the **same network** as the wired users.

**Connecting the dots:**

- The course goals include "explain how routers connect networks together." This is the first hint at that. A router sits between two networks and joins them.
- The IP address part ties to 2.3.2 (the router provides client IP addressing info) and 3.2.4 (my phone needs to get an IP address before it can use the Wi-Fi). It's the same router handing out addresses to everything on the local network, wired or wireless.

Here's how I'm picturing it:

```
Service provider (cable or DSL)
        |
     [ Modem ]          <- converts the provider's signals
        |
   [ Internet port ]
   |-------------------|
   |      ROUTER       |   <- sits between two networks
   |-------------------|
   [ LAN ports ] [ Wireless AP ]
        |              |
  wired devices   wireless devices
   (all on the same local network)
```

If the modem's built in, the modem and router are in one box.

## 4.1.2 Components of a home network

A home network isn't just the router. Besides an **integrated router**, lots of other devices can connect to it, like:

- Desktop computers
- Gaming systems
- Smart TV systems
- Printers
- Scanners
- Security cameras
- Telephones
- Climate control devices

And as new technology comes out, **more household functions** will use the network for **connectivity and control**.

The diagram in the course is labeled **Home Wireless Local Area Network (WLAN)**, which is just a LAN that's wireless.

**Connecting the dots:** this list matches 1.1.6 (connected home devices: security systems, appliances, smart TVs, gaming consoles). Those are all end devices that hang off this one router.

## 4.1.3 Typical home network routers

Small business and home routers usually have **two main types of ports**:

| Port | What it's for |
|---|---|
| **Ethernet port** | Connects to the router's **internal switch**. Usually labeled **"Ethernet"** or **"LAN."** All devices plugged into the switch ports are on the **same local network**. |
| **Internet port** | Connects the device to **another network**, a different one from the Ethernet ports. Often used to connect to the **cable or DSL modem** to get to the internet. |

**Connecting the dots:** this confirms my guess from the Module 4 README. The **Ethernet/LAN ports are the router's built-in switch**, which fits 2.3.2 (a wireless integrated router includes a switch for wired hosts and a wireless AP for wireless hosts). So Kishori's box in the corner has:

- an **internet port** (to the outside world)
- **Ethernet/LAN ports** (wired devices)
- a **wireless access point** (what she uses most)

## What I want to remember

- Most home networks are **two networks**: the provider's public network and my local network.
- A **modem** converts the provider's signals into something an Ethernet network can use. Some home devices have the modem **built in**.
- **Wireless routers** are called routers because they connect **two networks**: the local one (wired and wireless) and the one on the internet side.
- Wired and wireless devices usually share the **same local network**, so they get IP addresses on the same network.
- **Ethernet/LAN ports** connect to the router's internal **switch**. The **internet port** connects to a **different network**, often the cable or DSL modem.
- Lots of devices can be on a home network: computers, gaming systems, smart TVs, printers, cameras, and more. This is the **home WLAN**.
