## 3.1.1 Video: Types of wireless networks

So the big idea is that my phone isn't on *one* network. It's on a bunch of different ones, depending on what I'm doing.

- **Talking to someone on another cell phone:** the voice goes through the **cellular provider network**.
- **Using a Bluetooth speaker, headset, or smartwatch:** that's a **completely different network**. It's much **shorter range** and runs on **different rules and protocols**.
- **Wi-Fi:** probably the one I know best, since it's on tons of devices, phones included. Usually I'm using it to get onto the **global internet**.
- **Near field communication (NFC):** sends information over an **extremely short distance**. This is the "tap my phone to pay" thing at a grocery store or fast food place.
- **GPS:** my phone **receives signals from satellites** that orbit the Earth. With those, it figures out where it is, usually within a few meters.

All of those protocols, rules, and frequencies live **inside the same device**, and they can all be running **at the same time**. The guy in the video said it's pretty amazing, and honestly, I agree.

**Connecting the dots:** "different rules and protocols" is the same idea from the course goals about standards and protocols. Each of these networks has its own set of rules for how devices talk. That's why Bluetooth doesn't work like Wi-Fi.

## 3.1.2 Video: Cell phone interactions with different networks

This one flips it around. Instead of listing networks, it lists what I actually *do* on my phone, and then which network is behind it.

| What I'm doing | What's behind it |
|---|---|
| **Talking** (the phone's main job): calling landlines, other cell phones, and businesses | One of the phone's **radios** |
| **Using data** (what my data plan is for), like looking something up when I'm not near Wi-Fi | The **data plan network**, which uses the **cell phone infrastructure** to reach the internet |
| **Wi-Fi**: hotspots and my home wireless network | Wi-Fi |
| **Location services**: GPS, maps, "places near me" | GPS |
| **Paying**: holding my phone near the cash register or payment pad so my financial info transfers automatically | Pay services (NFC) |

The part that stuck with me: a cell phone has **multiple radios, transmitters, and receivers** in it. So it's not one antenna doing everything. It's more like a bunch of mini radios, each with a different job.

**Connecting the dots:** this lines up with 2.3.1 and 2.3.4. The cellular network works as an ISP (that's the "data plan network" here), and the carrier meters the data, which is why there's a data plan to begin with. Wi-Fi is the other way my phone gets online, usually through the wireless access point in a home router.

## 3.1.3 Other wireless networks

In addition to the **GSM and 4G/5G** transmitters and receivers (that's the cellular side, though the course doesn't explain GSM here), smartphones connect in some other ways too.

### GPS (Global Positioning System)

- **Satellites** send signals that cover the whole globe.
- The phone **receives** these signals and **calculates its location** to within about **10 meters**.

So the phone is the one listening and doing the math. (The video said "a few meters" and the reading says within 10 meters, so I'd say "pretty accurate, but not perfect.")

### Wi-Fi

- Wi-Fi transmitters and receivers in the phone let it connect to **local networks and the internet**.
- To send and receive data, the phone has to be **in range of a wireless access point**.
- Wi-Fi networks are usually **privately owned**, but they often offer **guest or public access hotspots**.
- A **hotspot** = an area where Wi-Fi signals are available.
- A Wi-Fi connection on a phone is similar to one on a laptop.

### Bluetooth

- **Low-power, shorter range** wireless tech.
- Meant to **replace wired connections** for accessories like speakers, headphones, and microphones.
- Can connect a **smartwatch to a smartphone**.
- It can carry **both data and voice**, so it can be used to build **small local networks**.
- **Multiple devices** can be connected at the same time.

### NFC (Near Field Communication)

- Lets devices exchange data when they're **very close**, usually **less than a few centimeters**.
- Example: connecting a smartphone to a **payment system**.
- Uses **electromagnetic fields** to send the data.

## Comparing them

| Technology | Mostly used for | Range | Fun fact |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GSM / 4G / 5G** | Calls, texts, mobile data | Wherever there's a cell signal | Works through the carrier's network |
| **GPS** | Finding location | Satellites cover the globe | The phone only receives |
| **Wi-Fi** | Local network and internet access | Within range of an access point | Usually privately owned, with some public hotspots |
| **Bluetooth** | Accessories (speakers, headphones, smartwatch) | Short | Low power, can connect several devices at once |
| **NFC** | Tap-to-pay | A few centimeters at most | Uses electromagnetic fields |

## What I want to remember

- A phone uses **many networks at once**: cellular, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, NFC, and GPS. Each has its own rules, protocols, and frequencies.
- The phone has **multiple radios** inside to handle all of them.
- **Cellular** = calls and data plan. **Wi-Fi** = local network and internet (needs to be in range of an access point). **Bluetooth** = short-range accessories. **NFC** = tap-to-pay within a few centimeters. **GPS** = location from satellite signals, accurate to within about 10 meters.
- A **hotspot** is an area where Wi-Fi signals are available.
