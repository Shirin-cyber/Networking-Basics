## 1.1.3 Who owns "the internet"?

Nobody. No single person, company, or group owns it.

What it actually is: a worldwide collection of networks connected to each other, all cooperating to share information using **common standards**. And now the name makes sense. "Internet" is short for **internetwork**, so it's literally networks connected to networks.

The information travels through a mix of:

- telephone wires
- fiber-optic cables
- wireless transmissions
- satellite links

And everything I access online (social media, multiplayer games, email, online courses) lives somewhere on this global internet. Each of those destinations is connected to a local network that sends and receives information through the internet.

**Connecting the dots:** if nobody owns it, and it's made of a ton of different networks, then the only thing keeping it from being total chaos is that everyone follows the same shared rules. ## 1.1.4 Local networks

Local networks come in all sizes, from just two computers all the way up to hundreds of thousands of devices.

The small ones, in homes or small offices, are called **SOHO networks** (small office/home office). They let a few people share things like printers, documents, pictures, and music.

Bigger networks are used by businesses to:

- advertise and sell products
- order supplies
- communicate with customers

It's usually faster and cheaper than the old ways, like regular mail or long-distance phone calls. Plus, you get stuff like email and instant messaging, and information can be stored in one place on network **servers** so everyone can reach it.

Most business and SOHO networks share one connection to the internet. And that's why the internet gets called a **"network of networks"**: it's literally thousands of local networks joined together. So even my home Wi-Fi is one small piece of the internet.

### The 4 sizes of network

| Type | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Small home network** | Connects a few computers to each other and to the internet |
| **Small office / home office (SOHO)** | Lets computers in a home office or remote office connect to a corporate network, or reach centralized shared resources |
| **Medium to large network** | Used by corporations and schools, with many locations and hundreds or thousands of interconnected hosts (I think "hosts" just means devices on the network) |
| **World wide network** | The internet, connecting hundreds of millions of computers around the world |

## 1.1.5 Mobile devices

It's not just desktops and laptops that connect to the internet anymore. There are a bunch of devices around us that do too.

- **Smartphone:** Can connect from almost anywhere. It combines a lot of things into one device: phone, camera, GPS, media player, and a touch screen computer.
- **Tablet:** Also does the job of multiple devices, but with a bigger screen, so it's great for videos, magazines, and books. With the on-screen keyboard you can do a lot of what you'd do on a laptop, like writing emails or browsing the web.
- **Smartwatch:** Connects to a smartphone to send you alerts and messages. It can also do things like track your heart rate and count steps, so people use it to keep an eye on their health.
- **Smart glasses:** A wearable computer shaped like glasses (Google Glass is the example). It has a tiny screen that shows info to the wearer, kind of like the heads-up display (HUD) a fighter pilot sees. There's a small touch pad on the side to move through menus while you can still see through them.

## 1.1.6 Connected home devices

Plenty of things in a house can connect to the internet too, so you can monitor and control them remotely.

- **Security system:** Security systems, lighting, and climate controls can be monitored and configured from a mobile device.
- **Appliances:** Fridges, ovens, and dishwashers can connect. You can turn them on or off, check their status, and get alerts when something hits a preset condition, like the fridge getting warmer than it should.
- **Smart TV:** Gets content straight from the internet, so you don't need equipment from a TV service provider. You can also browse the web, write email, or play video, audio, and photos that are stored on a computer.
- **Gaming console:** Connects to download games and play with friends online.

## 1.1.7 Other connected devices

Outside the house, there are also connected devices that are convenient, and sometimes even vital.

- **Smart cars:** Modern cars can connect to the internet for maps, audio, video, or info about a destination. They can send a text or email if someone tries to steal the car, or call for help after an accident. They can also link up with a phone or tablet to show engine info, maintenance alerts, and the security system status.
- **RFID tags:** Radio frequency identification tags can go in or on objects to track them or monitor sensors for different conditions.
- **Sensors and actuators:** Sensors can report things like temperature, humidity, wind speed, air pressure, and soil moisture. **Actuators** are the part that does something automatically based on that data.
- **Medical devices:** Pacemakers, insulin pumps, and hospital monitors give users or medical staff direct feedback or alerts when vital signs hit certain levels.

### The sensor and actuator example (it's a loop)

This one was easier to get once I laid it out step by step:

1. A soil sensor sends moisture data to a monitoring station every so often.
2. When it's dry enough, the station sends a signal to an actuator to start watering.
3. The sensor keeps sending moisture data while that's happening.
4. The station sees when the soil is wet enough and tells the actuator to turn off.

So the sensor is the eyes, the actuator is the hands, and the monitoring station is the brain deciding what happens.

**Connecting the dots:** this goes back to Kishori from the module intro. The network-connected hospital equipment she uses at work is exactly this kind of thing. If a monitor stops talking to the network, someone could miss an alert. That's why it matters so much that all these devices communicate properly.
