## 1.2.1 So what even is data?

People say "data" constantly when they talk about networks: data being transmitted, entered, stored, and then there's "big data" sitting somewhere in the cloud. But what is it, really?

In its raw form, data is just **the information you put in**. Every time I hit send, share, or upload, I'm putting data I created out onto the network.

### The 3 types of data

| Type | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Volunteered data** | Data I offer up myself. I know it's being collected, and I'm agreeing to share or store it on the network. | Posting a picture, filling out a form |
| **Inferred data** | Data that gets generated by my activities, so I might not realize it's being collected. | Credit card use: every place I swipe keeps a record, so over time they can pick up my preferences, where I usually eat or shop, and even roughly where I am |
| **Observed data** | Data collected by watching what my devices do. | My phone keeps track of my location, and that info is stored and sent to my carrier |

The line between inferred and observed felt a bit blurry to me. What stuck is the difference between volunteered and the other two: with volunteered data, I **chose** to hand it over. With the other two, it's collected from what I do, whether I'm thinking about it or not.

All of that adds up to a huge amount of information being collected and stored, which is where the term **big data** in the cloud comes from.

**Takeaway:** I should think a bit before I click share or store, so I actually know where my stuff is going.

## 1.2.2 The bit

This part blew my mind a little. Computers and networks only work with **binary digits: zeros and ones**. Every photo, song, message, and video I've ever sent is, at the bottom, just a long string of 0s and 1s.

- A **bit** is short for "binary digit." It's the smallest piece of data, and it can only be **0 or 1**.
- Humans understand words and pictures. Computers only understand **patterns of bits**.

### How can a bit be stored or sent?

As one of two distinct states. It could be:

- two directions of magnetization
- two different voltage or current levels
- two different levels of light intensity
- any physical system that has two clear states

The easiest way to picture it is a light switch. **On = 1, Off = 0.**

### Input and output devices

So how do I get from typing on a keyboard to bits?

- **Input devices** (mouse, keyboard, voice-activated receiver) translate what I do into binary code, so the CPU can process and store it.
- **Output devices** (printer, speakers, monitor) take the binary data and translate it back into something I can understand.
- Inside the computer itself, everything is processed and stored as binary.

### Turning letters and numbers into bits: ASCII

Computers use codes to represent letters, numbers, and special characters with bits. A really common one is **ASCII** (American Standard Code for Information Interchange). In ASCII, each character takes **8 bits**.

| Character | Binary |
|---|---|
| Capital **A** | `01000001` |
| Number **9** | `00111001` |
| Special character **#** | `00100011` |

A group of 8 bits like these is called a **byte**.

**Connecting the dots:** if one character is one byte (8 bits), then a 5-letter word is 5 bytes, or 40 bits. It also means the "9" I type is not stored as the number nine. It's stored as a pattern of bits that the computer reads as the character 9.

Codes like this aren't just for text. They can represent almost anything digitally: computer data, graphics, photos, voice, video, and music.

## 1.2.3 How does data get sent?

Once my data is a series of bits, it has to be turned into **signals** so it can travel across the network **media** to wherever it's going.

- **Media** = the physical thing the signals travel through. For example: copper wire, fiber-optic cable, or electromagnetic waves through the air.
- **Signal** = electrical or optical patterns sent from one connected device to another. The patterns represent the bits, and they travel from source to destination as **pulses of electricity, pulses of light, or radio waves**.

Signals can be converted a bunch of times on the way, because the media can change between source and destination.

### The 3 common methods

| Method | How it works | Media |
|---|---|---|
| **Electrical signals** | Data is represented as electrical pulses | Copper wire |
| **Optical signals** | Electrical signals get converted into light pulses | Fiber-optic cable |
| **Wireless signals** | Data is sent using infrared, microwave, or radio waves | Through the air |

### What's used where

- **Homes and small businesses:** usually copper wires (cables) or Wi-Fi wireless.
- **Larger networks:** use fiber-optic cables to carry signals reliably over longer distances.

**Connecting the dots:** since signals can change form along the way, one message I send could probably start as radio waves (Wi-Fi from my phone), turn into electrical pulses on a cable, then into light on a fiber line, and get converted back again near the destination. It's still the same bits the whole time, just in different forms. This also lines up with the "describe common network media" goal from the course intro, so it'll come back later.

## What I want to remember

- Data is the information I put in. There are three kinds: **volunteered**, **inferred**, and **observed**.
- A **bit** is a 0 or 1, and **8 bits make a byte**.
- Computers only understand bits, and codes like **ASCII** turn characters into bits.
- Bits travel as **electrical**, **optical**, or **wireless** signals through network media.
- Copper and Wi-Fi are common in homes and small businesses, and fiber is used in larger networks for long distances.
